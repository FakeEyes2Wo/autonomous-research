Biometrics Computer Vision Object Recognition Visual Perception Machine Learning Artificial Intelligence Deep Learning Approaches in Face Recognition 1 Introduction Variable recognizability of humans is the concept that some may be easily distinguished where others can fade into a crowd. “Doddington’s Zoo” [ 1 , 2 ] is a taxonomy of the recognizability of human beings, where individuals are categorised into different zoomorphic groups based on how easily they can be distinguished or recognized. Principally, a large proportion of individuals can be tagged as highly recognisable, or ”Sheep” in the taxonomy, where only a small amount fall into the ”difficult to recognise” group. However, this small population will disproportionately contribute to the error rate of an automatic recognition system. In addition, for the best recognisable population it may not be necessary to process their biometric data into complex descriptions, as simpler feature vectors may suffice. In addition, if the model is trained with a biased data set, this bias will naturally manifest in the models ability to distinguish certain cohorts of individuals. One state of the art deep learning model for vision tasks is the Vision Transformer (ViT) [ 3 ]. ViT's obviate the usage of convolutions and instead use multi-headed self-attention (MHSA) to encode an image. MHSA has been shown to be an extremely powerful operation, with similar expressive power to convolution, and has some similarities with human vision [ 4 , 5 ]. ViTs generally have shown state of the art performance in a wide range of applications such as image classification [ 3 ], object detection [ 6 ] and text to image generation [ 7 ]. Early exit (EE) approaches in machine learning are one solution for implementing time critical applications on resource-constrained devices. In these instances compute budget is something which must be considered. EE systems allow a machine learning process to use only a portion of the full network where using the full network would be either unnecessary or infeasible. They have found applications in computing continuum applications where earlier exits of the model are computed on edge devices and later exits can be offloaded to the cloud [ 8 ]. EE approaches fundamentally reduce the compute burden of a model while minimising the effect on inference performance. The smoothness of this reduction with respect to bias is yet to be studied, there is some suggestion that reductions in model representative capacity can exacerbate bias present in the training data [ 9 ]. Due to various high-profile cases and media attention, bias is now a central concern of biometric recognition. Algorithms are expected to be inherently impartial in the way data is processed, making no difference in their functionality regardless of the subject. Considering biometric identification, where the system is expected to perceive individual differences, this is even more true. Fairness in biometric identification entails impartiality, where no bias or preference is shown for any individual or group, based on inherent or acquired characteristics. Often in deep learning models, as the performance is determined by characteristics of the training data, unfairness is due to bias. Primarily due to the lower performance, often demonstrated in matching ethnic minorities, bias in biometrics is subject to much critical attention, especially by the media. Another fundamental concept in this space is the “other race effect”, the well-studied phenomenon where both Humans [ 10 ] and ML models [ 11 , 12 ] have more difficulty differentiating individual elements of a cohort they have less experience of. This is generally observed with respect to race, hence the other race effect , where individuals have a bias towards recognising their own race. Where this effect in humans has both a genotypic and a phenotypic base [ 10 ], in ML models it is widely considered to be related to the data the model is presented, though it should be noted that algorithmic bias may play a part and disentangling these is non-trivial. In the context of this study, our objective is to elucidate the following fundamental questions. 1: Can we exploit recognisability with Early Exits to reduce the compute burden of a face recognition model without significantly affecting the performance? 2: How do these early exits interact in a biased model trained on an unbalanced data set? 3: Can EEs show how bias manifests generally at the matching score level? 4: Can we mitigate the bias with an appropriate strategy?. In this paper, we extend the work of [ 13 ] by generalizing the approach to encompass multiple configurations of EEs. We take a standard ViT, incorporate additional EEs and train multiple models with respect to these. We explore a novel application of percentile based exit criterion on a closed set gallery, allowing these criterion to be computed ad-hoc, only dependent on the outputs of an ML model. We present how EEs added to a ViT, combined with our exit criterion, can reduce the compute budget at inference with only a small loss in performance. We then investigate the relationship between the EEs and the bias in the model, through a robust evaluation of matching scores across a racially balanced data set. The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. First, in Sect. 2 , we detail the relevant background to our work. Next, in Sect. 3 , we present the model, EEs, data and evaluation metrics for our work. Next, in Sect. 4 , we present the results of our investigation, first with face recognition (Sect. 4.1 ) and then with bias (Sects. 4.2 , 4.3 , 4.4 , 4.5 ). Finally, in Sect. 5 , we draw conclusions, outlining some additional research directions. 2 Related works There are many techniques for reducing the compute budget of a deep network, including network pruning [ 14 ], distillation [ 15 ] and quantisation [ 16 ]. Early Exits (EE) are another form in the group of dynamic inference (DI) techniques. DI modulates the complexity of an ML model in relation to some constraint (e.g., compute budget and energy consumption) or to reduce complexity where it offers little benefit. The final goal is to provide a result similar to the final result of the neural network while only using a subset of the layers of the full model. In general, accuracy is reduced but with a worthwhile reduction in computational cost. In [ 17 ], the authors study the effect of adding EE to any ML model, proposing a general framework that systematically “elastifies” an arbitrary network. There are also DI techniques which approach the problem from the opposite direction. [ 18 ] allows inputs for the “easier” images to be processed at a lower resolution. The authors offer a cascade of Transformers with varying token input counts, allowing the bulk of input images to be processed less, where the higher representational capacity models are reserved for the more challenging inputs. The most similar to our work is that of [ 19 , 20 ]. They proposed a metric-learning EE methodology for deep learning models applied to face verification. Three EEs were placed after the residual blocks of a ResNet-50 [ 21 ], trained on the large-scale MS-Celeb-1 M data set [ 22 ]. The approach reduced computational complexity while maintaining similar accuracy to the final output of the network. With respect to EE one of the drawbacks of CNNs is that the descriptive capability of earlier layers is challenging to express. This is due to the feature maps remaining wide with limited channel dimension. For example a ResNet-18 has feature map dimension 56*56*64 ( H*W*C) after the first block compared to 7*7*512 at the final block. Obviously, average pooling in the HW dimension loses a significantly higher amount of information at the earlier layers compared to the later. ViTs instead have uniform scale throughout processing, and while this may at first glance appear to be a disadvantage in terms of robustness to scale, these models have been shown to be sensitive to scale even without the prior embedded [ 3 ]. In the context of our work, this means that EEs can be operated uniformly throughout the model. EEs have been explored in ViT. In [ 23 ] seven different architectures for EE branches that can be inserted into ViT backbones are proposed. In [ 24 ] the authors propose a novel hybrid approach using Transformer-based EE branches on CNN backbones, terming them single-layer vision transformer (SL-ViT). We instead determine our exit criterion based on the matching scores of a gallery of identities, where the above methods balance computational budget against accuracy loss with the trained classifiers of the model. With respect to bias in FR systems currently there is a significant effort towards developing fair, transparent, and accountable AI technologies for facial analysis applications. There are many works and methodologies to measure and alleviate bias in the data as well in the computational models [ 25 , 26 , 27 ]. The study in [ 26 ] examined two significant data sets, IJB-A and Adience, and uncovered that they are primarily composed of individuals with lighter skin tones, accounting for 79.6% in IJB-A and 86.2% in Adience. Furthermore, an evaluation was conducted on three commercial gender classification systems, revealing a discriminative behavior. Lighter-skinned males exhibited an error rate of just 0.8%, whereas darker-skinned females experienced the highest misclassification, with error rates as high as 34.7%. They also investigate an approach to assess bias in phenotypic subgroups for both face data sets and models is proposed. The skin type is annotated using the six-point Fitzpatrick phenotype classification system, the gold standard for skin classification by dermatologists [ 28 ], ranging from lighter to darker. Data bias is determined simply by counting the number of subjects for each class label. Finally, they balance an evaluation data set, the misclassification rates per skin colour indicate the bias level of the model. Another work is that of [ 9 ]. Here, the authors explore the impact of model quantization on the augmentation of bias in the final model. They delve into how quantizing models, a technique commonly employed to reduce memory footprint or accelerate inference, may magnify bias into the model's predictions or representations. In [ 29 ], experiments were conducted on various commercial face matchers to assess why disparities in face recognition accuracy arise. For all the matchers under consideration, both the impostor and genuine distributions exhibit statistically significant differences between cohorts. Specifically, for a fixed decision threshold, the African-American image cohort demonstrates a higher false match rate and a lower false non-match rate, where the Caucasian displayed the inverse behaviour [ 29 ]. 3 Methods In this section, we present the methods of our work. We begin by discussing models we have developed, the process for training and the process for evaluation. In Sect. 3.1 , we discuss how the EEs applied to the model can be effectively used. Next, in Sects. 3.2 , 3.3 , 3.4 , we present all data used for training and evaluation of the model. Finally, in Sects. 3.5 , 3.6 , 3.7 , we present the metrics we use to evaluate the model. To begin, we start with an off-the-shelf ViT from HuggingFace [ 30 ] (specifically google/vit-base-patch16-224 ), we then add classifiers (exits) at intermediate layers, such that the model learns to generate discriminative embeddings at these layers. These classifiers are simple d*n linear layers where d is the hidden dimension of the ViT (in this case 768), and n is the number of classes in the training set. We investigate ViTs with 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6 exits as the ViT has 12 layers and these exit counts allow for equal spacing. We do not investigate a configuration with 12 exits due to lack of available compute resources. The EEs are placed starting from the final layer of the model, as such a ViT with 1 exit corresponds to standard ViT operation, where the loss is computed only at the final layer. A model with 6 exits will have classifiers placed after layers 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12 and the loss will be calculated in aggregate across these. The architecture of our system including an example with 6 exits is shown in Fig. 1 . Fig. 1 Full size image Vision transformer with six exits. a Standard ViT. b 5 EEs added to the base model. c ViT encoder To train the models we retain cross entropy loss to maintain vicinity to the majority of ViT literature, however, we adapt it to incorporate the additional classifiers. There are many strategies for combining losses in EE models [ 20 , 31 ], we simply use the average loss of all the classifiers for the backward pass. For a model with m exits, the EE cross entropy loss \({\text{L}}_{\text{CE}}^{\text{EE}}\) is then defined in the following equation: $${L}_{CE}^{EE}(T,p)= -\frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{T}_{i}^{j}\text{log}({p}_{i}^{j})$$ (1) where \({\text{T}}_{\text{i}}^{\text{j}}\) or \({\text{p}}_{\text{i}}^{\text{j}}\) refers to the target or predicted probability, respectively, of class i \((1\,\,, &lt; \,i\, \le \,n)\) at exit j \((1\, &lt; \,j\, \le m)\) . All models are transfer learned from weights pre-trained on ImageNet-1k [ 32 ]. Transfer learning from a general purpose, large scale data set such as ImageNet to other tasks has been shown to significantly speed up training, as well as some studies suggesting models pre-trained on ImageNet generalise as well, if not better, than models trained from scratch on a specific task [ 33 , 34 ]. In addition, this allows us to utilise a large portion of the approximately 2500 days of TPU processing performed by Google [ 3 ], further increasing the energy-efficiency of this system. Once training is complete, we discard all classifiers and use embeddings produced at the relevant layers for matching. To match two images, we pass the images to the model, extract the embeddings from a single exit and compare these with Cosine similarity, we only ever compare embeddings extracted from the same exit. The Cosine similarity between a pair of image embeddings \(\overrightarrow{A}\) and \(\overrightarrow{B}\) is defined as: $$cossim\,(\vec{A}\,\vec{B}) = \,\frac{\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B}}{\mid\vec{A}\mid \mid\vec{B}\mid}$$ (2) Cosine similarity is the only comparative metric we use. For the remainder of this article, we refer to this as the score between two feature vectors. 3.1 Exit criterion In [ 2 ], the taxonomy of the recognizability of humans defined by [ 1 ] was used to improve the performance of a biometric system. Within the taxonomy there are various zoomorphic categories which define the recognizability characteristics of a person. These categories can be utilised to determine exit criterion in a system with a closed-set gallery. The simplest and most commonly occurring category of recognizability is the Sheep : an identity which exhibits high genuine matching scores and low imposter matching scores. Practically, this is a person who is easy to recognise. Another category we consider is the Goat which exhibits low genuine match scores, i.e., these are less likely to be recognised correctly. Finally, we consider Lambs which have high impostor scores, these are more likely to be recognised incorrectly. To determine if an identity is a Goat, we first compute the mean genuine score for that identity \({\text{S}}_{\text{GEN}}\) . If this identity's \({\text{S}}_{\text{GEN}}\) is below a threshold, then the identity is classified as a Goat ; otherwise, it is either a Sheep or a Lamb . To determine if an identity is a Lamb, we compute the maximum impostor score between the current identity and every other identity \({\overrightarrow{\text{M}}}_{\text{IMP}}^{\text{k}}\) k = 1…n, where n is the number of identities. We then take the mean of \({\overrightarrow{\text{M}}}_{\text{IMP}}^{\text{k}}\) to form the mean impostor score for this identity \({\text{S}}_{\text{IMP}}\) in the following equation: $$\text{S}_{\text{IMP}} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k = 1}^n\overrightarrow{\text{M}}_{IMP}^{k}$$ (3) If \(\text{S}_{\text{IMP}}\) is above a threshold, then the identity is classified as having high impostor scores, and is a Lamb . Should an identity not pass either the Goat or the Lamb test, then it is considered a Sheep . Should an identity be simultaneously a Goat and a Lamb it is considered a Goat as we compute this first. While in Doddington's taxonomy there are a much larger number of categories (worms, wolves, doves etc..) we will focus on the three defined above. The exit thresholds are determined by percentiles at each exit. For the first exit in a model, we compute \(\text{S}_{\text{GEN}}\) and \({\text{S}}_{\text{IMP}}\) for all users. We then choose a pair of Goat/Lamb percentiles and compute the thresholds \({\text{T}}_{\text{GOAT}}\) and \({\text{T}}_{\text{LAMB}}\) at that percentile over \({\text{S}}_{\text{GEN}}\) and \({\text{S}}_{\text{IMP}}\) . We then reject users who's \({\text{S}}_{\text{GEN}}\) or \({\text{S}}_{\text{IMP}}\) is lower or higher, respectively. For instance, if the Goat percentile is 60th and the Lamb percentile is 50th, then the identities with the lowest 60% of \({\text{S}}_{\text{GEN}}\) and the highest 50% of \({\text{S}}_{\text{IMP}}\) are rejected by this exit and passed to the next. \({\text{S}}_{\text{GEN}}\) and \({\text{S}}_{\text{IMP}}\) are then recalculated at the next exit for all rejected identities (with the new exit's embeddings). This process is repeated until the final layer where all remaining identities are accepted. Figure 2 demonstrates this. Fig. 2 Full size image Visual representation of exit criterion In our experiments, we sweep the percentiles from the zeroth to the 100th percentile in increments of 10. We do this for both \({\text{T}}_{\text{GOAT}}\) and \({\text{T}}_{\text{LAMB}}\) , resulting in 121 different threshold combinations. 3.2 Data and fine-tuning We begin with models pre-trained on ImageNet-1k, append EEs to the appropriate layers, and fine-tune on CASIA-WebFace [ 35 ], a data set of 494,414 face images of 10,575 identities. We train each model for 80 epochs on 95% of the data set, leaving 5% for validation. We use the AdamW optimiser with a learning rate of \(1{\text{e}}^{-4}\) , decayed according to a cosine schedule, a weight decay of \(1{\text{e}}^{-2}\) and default betas. We retain the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss across all training for evaluation. All models converged between epoch 60 and 65. We discard the uncontrolled examples to remove as much noise as possible from the testing set, we also discard any identities with less than ten images. We do this to ensure that we have a high quality evaluation dataset, where other wild image characteristics will not contribute confounding information to the evaluation. We focus purely on the recognizability of an individual in controlled conditions, where the wild images used during fine-tuning ensure a robust representation. What results is a curated set of 24,120 images of 472 identities. We then extract the face from each image at two times the width of the eyes, offset vertically by 0.3 times eye width to remove as much background as possible and approximately center the faces. We split this subset of FRGC into a 30/70 (7236/16884 images) probe/gallery set. This is approximately 122 million probe/gallery and 285 million gallery/gallery comparisons. To reduce the total volume of comparisons, and through empirical testing we have seen it makes little difference to the results, we sample approximately 10 million gallery/gallery pairs to compute exit identities, and approximately 5 million probe/gallery pairs to present results. In both cases we ensure approximate identity-size-parity between the sampled pairs and the original data set, and that approximately 10% of pairs are matches. 3.4 Bias evaluation data For our Bias experiments, we use Racial Faces in the Wild (RFW) [ 37 ]. RFW is a face data set containing 40,607 images balanced across race, additionally incorporating identity. We chose this data set first because of the balanced racial representation, and second because it provides identities and testing pairs. Other data sets considered included FairFace [ 38 ] and Labelled Faces in the Wild + Attributes (LFWA) [ 39 ]. LFWA was discarded due to being smaller, not balanced, and with noisy attribute labels. FairFace was discarded because despite containing far more images than RFW and being balanced across Race, Gender and Age, it does not provide identities. In this section we present the two sets of metrics used for our results. 3.6 Face recognition metrics For our face recognition experiments we use two different evaluation metrics to compare performance between exits and between models. First, we use the Area Under Curve (AUC). This is the area under the Receiver Operator Curve (ROC), and measures the rate at which your system, independent of threshold, will correctly order positive and negative pairs. Second, we use True Match Rate at False Match Rate of 0.1% (TMr@FMr0.1%). This measures the true match rate of the model when the false match rate is fixed at 0.1%. 3.7 Bias evaluation metrics For bias the metrics we use the Mean Inter-Class Similarity (the distribution of genuine scores) and the Score Overlap Error both within and between cohorts. These are visualised in Fig. 4 . Fig. 4 Full size image Visualisation of metrics for evaluating bias Both of these metrics are calculated as aggregate for a given protocol. First, the Score Overlap Error (shown in turquoise in Fig. 4 ) is calculated by locating the point of intersection between the impostor and genuine curves, and then counting the number of genuine points and the number of impostor points lower and higher, respectively. This is then represented as the percentage of the total area covered by the genuine and impostor distributions. This metric informs us of the area of the score graph where the False Negatives and the False Positives simultaneously exist. Larger values indicate a more severe overlap, and should correlate with lower recognition rates on average. The Mean Genuine Score and Mean Inter-Class Similarity (shown in orange in Fig. 4 ) offer an understanding of the positioning and shape of the genuine score distribution. Higher mean genuine score combined with low mean inter-class variability indicates a well-defined set of high genuine scores. Higher inter-class variability indicates a lower descriptiveness of the feature vector, as the genuine scores are more dispersed. 4 Results In this section, we present our results. We then compare these to the right hand sides of the impostor pairs from the other 3 cohorts. This results in 300 genuine comparisons and 900 impostor comparisons. Figure 7 shows our results. Fig. 7 Full size image Graphs of score comparisons for the four races of RFW, at each EE of our 6 exit model. The graphs show within class genuine, within class overlap, between class overlap and between all classes overlap. Score overlap is expressed as the percentage of the total area covered by the Genuine and Impostor distributions The first observation is that from Fig. 7 b, the African cohort clearly has a significantly higher overlap when compared within race vs the other races, indicating that their genuine and impostor distributions are more similar. The Indian cohort in Fig. 7 b has better separation, despite being by a significant margin the least represented cohort in our training set (see Fig. 6 ). For between class measurement, the African cohort have a clear positive separation from the other classes (Fig. 7 c), and when all classes are considered the performance reduces again (Fig. 7 d). As our results are from a balanced data set, it is then clear that if your evaluation data set is not balanced and your results are aggregate, that poor within cohort results can be masked by excellent between cohort scoring. This between cohort scoring is largely irrelevant: we do not need an automatic face matching system to match between evidently racially different subjects. With respect to the EEs we can see that our model magnifies these results at the earlier exits. From Fig. 7 b, the difference between within class overlap of Caucasian and African is ~ 0.58 at layer 2 of the model, where it is ~ 0.38 at the final exit. This supports and expands the work of [ 9 ]: in their work, they find that increased quantisation of a model increases the racial bias of the model's decisions (as discussed in Sect. 2 ). Our experiment expands upon this showing that that our different form of reduction in a model's representative capacity increases the propensity for biased decisions. Principally, as our training set contains a majority of Caucasian subjects one would expect our model to perform best for Caucasian subjects. This is shown in Fig. 7 where they have by far the lowest within class overlap at each exit, where the African cohort have the highest. Interestingly, when we consider the inter-class variance in Fig. 7 a, the African cohort have the highest genuine scores, with an approximately similar variance to the other cohorts, supporting the findings of [ 29 ] (as discussed in Sect. 2 ). We have observed in our experiments that less represented, in the training data, cohorts tend to exit at the earlier stages of the model. Operating under the assumption that RFW's African and FRGC's Black-or-African-American refer to similar racial characteristics, this explains why the Black-or-African-American cohort of FRGC exits on average earlier. These cohorts have higher genuine scores, and due to the racial imbalance of FRGC their impostor separations are artificially inflated. What results is that individuals from these cohorts are naturally placed at the earlier exits where they are discriminated significantly more poorly than the latter. This suggests that the EE criterion we have in place are sensitive to a racially biased model, and in some cases can magnify the issue. More promisingly it suggests that the full potential of EE's could be unlocked by training on a balanced data set. We explore this in Sects. 4.4 and 4.5 . In this section, we will investigate two strategies for balancing our training, and evaluate their effect on the resulting bias. First, we will train our 6 exit model on a balanced data set, namely, BUPT–BalancedFace [ 43 ]. This data set contains 1,251,416 images of 28,000 identities with equal identity and image count across the same four racial cohorts of RFW. Second, we train our 6 exit model again on CASIA–WebFace, however we instead weight the loss inversely to the racial proportion of the data set. We will use the estimates provided by DeepFace shown in Fig. 6 and weight each identity inversely to its cohort proportion in the data set. Figure 10 shows the score distributions from RFW extracted with our 6 exit model trained on BUPT–BalancedFace. We can see immediately that the variation between the Caucasian, Indian and Asian cohorts is significantly reduced with respect to the model in Sects. 4.2 and 4.3 . In addition, as BUPT–BalancedFace contains approximately 2.5× the number of images, the average error and similarity is reduced across the board due to the larger training data set. There is still clearly some bias, where the Caucasian cohort still have clear stronger performance when matching within cohort. What remains is the African cohort, which despite an even representation in the training data, still has significantly better between cohort, and significantly worse within cohort matching scores. The model continues to have difficulty extracting robust, meaningful facial descriptions from these images. This implies that unbalanced training in-fact is not the sole-cause of biased decisions in the model, and that specific characteristics of the data themselves are also introducing partiality. Finally, we can observe that the magnification effect present in Sect. 4.2 , where earlier exits exhibited higher performance differentials between cohorts than the later exits, is attenuated in this case. The African cohort still performs significantly worse within-race, and significantly better between race, while the Caucasian cohort performs much better within race than all others. Unlike Fig. 7 , the Indian cohort has significantly more varied performance by exit in the within race experiment. We hypothesize that this is due to the notable under-representation of the Indian cohort in Casia–WebFace, and the resultantly high loss weight, causing overfitting. We can see that performance on Caucasian subjects is significantly improved with respect to the other races, where in Fig. 10 , the performance of the Caucasian cohort is much more inline with the Asian and Indian cohorts. Both balancing the loss and balancing the data set appear to have the same issue with respect to the African cohort. The African cohort has significantly better between-cohort matching, and significantly worse within-cohort matching across all experiments. This supports the previous implications that some characteristic of the data is impacting the ability of the model to extract meaningful, robust facial features from the African cohort. The cohort balance within the training data set is clearly a meaningful component of the manifestation of the bias, evidently however, it is not the complete story. 5 Conclusions In this paper, we have presented an approach based on the Vision Transformer and Early Exits for reducing compute budget without significantly affecting performance. Building on the work in [ 13 ], we have extended the recognition to multiple exit configurations, and we have analysed how the exits interact with bias, trying to answer the four questions: 1: Can we exploit recognisability with EEs to reduce the compute burden of a face recognition model without significantly affecting the performance? 2: How do these early exits interact in a biased model trained on an unbalanced data set? 3: Can EEs show how bias manifests generally at the matching score level? and 4: Can we mitigate the bias with an appropriate strategy? With respect to question 1, from our results in Sect. 4.1 we have shown a 18.2% reduction in compute performance at the cost of only 1% TMr@FMr0.1%. We can clearly observe a compute benefit using the EEs, however reducing compute below 20% can cause a significant reduction in recognition performance. In addition, with our current exit criterion, it would appear that error is more likely to be propagated once the number of exits increases. With respect to questions 2 and 3, we have shown that the training on a biased data set has manifested in our models ability to match different racial cohorts of individuals. The early exits show stronger divergence in quality of decision the earlier they are placed in the model. These can be considered analogous to lower-representational capacity models, which have been shown to exacerbate bias. Results show that due to the nature of how bias manifests, with overconfident genuine scores and good between cohort impostor scores for poorly represented cohorts, that the current implementation of exit criterion may in fact exacerbate the bias in the model. This does have the implication that a balanced training data set may unlock the full potential of EEs when applied to face recognition. With respect to question 4, we have shown that bias can be mitigated at all exits of the model for certain cohorts, however there remains some uncertainty as to the poor performance of others. Using a balanced data set approximately equalises the performance between the Asian, Indian and Caucasian cohorts of RFW, with the African cohort exhibiting similar performance characteristics as before. With loss balancing, we observe less effect, with similar results to unbalanced training. Although investigating the specific cause of this is outside of the scope of this article, we speculate that some characteristic of the data is the cause of this effect. With respect to our work we have some future steps we would like to take. First, we would like to investigate the effect of EEs on other Transformer architectures. We would also like to return to our loss function, as likely there is performance to be gained. ArcFace, triplet loss and others are avenues we would like to explore. Second, as the biased models tend to make overconfident decisions with relation to the poorly represented cohorts, we would like to consider progressive measures for reducing the strength of decisions made by models based on the data they are trained on and the progress of training. Another investigation we would like to consider is to learn to offset scores based on cohort labels, to mitigate this overconfidence.
